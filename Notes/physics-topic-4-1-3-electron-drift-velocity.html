Physics Topic 4.1.3 - Electron Drift Velocity

The Crystalline Structure Of Metals

When copper is examined, it is found to be crystalline, meaning the electrons are free to move through randomly through the lattice (delocalised/conduction electrons). These are what makes metals such good conductors. While conducting, the conduction electrons drift slowly down the wire. Electricity is "instant", because the electrons already at the device start moving as soon as PD is applied, not moving from the switch to the device.
The movement can be described as random, when there is no potential difference along the wire. This means there is no net negative movement of electrons.
When PD (Physics Topic 4.2.1 - Potential Difference And E.M.F) is applied across a wire, a current flows. The random motion is still present, but now there is a net movement of electrons, from the negative potential, towards the positive potential (the opposite direction to conventional current, see Physics Topic 4.1.1 - Electric Circuit Components).

Drift Velocity

The mean drift velocity is the subject of the formula \(v = \frac{I}{(nAe)}\) (from \(vnAe = I\)), where v is the mean drift velocity, I is the current flowing in amps, n is the number of free electrons per unit of volume, A is the cross sectional area of the wire, and e is the charge on the electron (Physics Topic 4.1.2 - Electric Current And Charge). For a fixed current, v is inversely proportional to A, so a thinner wire means faster drift, giving the \(y=\frac{k}{x}\) shape in Maths Topic 4.3 - Reciprocal Graphs.

Comparing Conductors, Semiconductors And Insulators

Metals have many free electrons, so are good conductors. Insulators have basically 0. Semiconductors have less than metals, but more than insulators. They undergo doping to improve or harm their conducting properties. Semiconductors are the basis of the components inside a processor, see Computer Science Topic 1 - Processor Components.
The factors that change resistance, such as the material, are in Physics Topic 4.2.2 - Resistance And Ohm's Law. Semiconductors are also the basis of solid state storage, see Computer Science Topic 6 - Storage Devices. Diodes and LEDs, which are made from semiconductors, are in Physics Topic 4.2.3 - Resistance Of Circuit Components.
